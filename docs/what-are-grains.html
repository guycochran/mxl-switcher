MXL in plain English

What's a "grain"? And what is a $400 computer actually doing?

The whole system is built out of one tiny idea. Once you get it, everything else — the cut, the multiview, the health numbers below — follows. No broadcast background needed.

A grain is one frame

Think of an old film reel: a long strip of still pictures. Play them fast enough — 30 a second — and your eye sees smooth motion. A "grain" is one of those pictures. One frame of video. (Audio works the same way: a grain is a short block of sound.)

So a normal 1080p camera running at 30 frames a second is producing 30 video grains every second, forever, as long as it's on.

A flow is one source

String all the grains from one camera together in order and you have a flow — the continuous stream of frames coming from that camera. Camera 1 is a flow. The playback clip is a flow. The guest on their phone is a flow. Your switcher is really just a room full of flows.

The clever part (MXL's whole reason to exist): normally, to send one camera to five different places you'd make five copies of the video — expensive. MXL instead puts each flow in shared memory — one copy that everything on the computer can read at once, like a whiteboard everyone in the room can see. The fifth reader costs almost nothing. That's why a cheap computer can do so much: it isn't copying video over and over.

Why we count grains — and unique grains

If you want to know a camera is healthy, you count its grains per second. Seeing a steady 30/s means frames are arriving on time. Simple.

But there's a sneakier failure. Sometimes a feed keeps delivering 30 grains a second while showing the same frozen picture — the counter ticks, but nothing moves. So we also measure how many of those frames are actually different. When "frames arriving" stays high but "frames actually changing" drops to near zero, we know the picture is frozen even though the plumbing looks fine. That single trick catches a whole class of live-TV disasters before anyone on air notices.

The point, for the skeptics

Broadcast infrastructure has always meant specialized, expensive hardware. The numbers above are the argument against that: an entire live production control room — ingest, switching, graphics, encoding and remote control — fits on a computer that costs less than a single pro SDI cable run, with CPU to spare. The grains flow through shared memory, nothing is needlessly copied, and commodity silicon keeps up.
